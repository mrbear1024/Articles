# This Is the Age of Great Falsification

Have you noticed that the "truths" you've believed in your entire life are being overturned by reality, one by one?

Study hard and you'll find a good job — falsified. Get into a big tech company and you've won at life — falsified. Buying a house is the best investment — falsified. Learn a trade and you'll eat for a lifetime — falsified. Pass the civil service exam and you've got an iron rice bowl — falsified. The higher the degree, the more you're worth — falsified.

It's not just one of these falling apart. **Nearly every foundational assumption you once relied on to make life decisions is being falsified simultaneously.**

In the philosophy of science, there's a concept called "falsification" — Karl Popper said that the value of a theory lies not in whether it can be "proven," but in whether it can be "falsified." When reality keeps slapping your theory in the face with facts, it's not the world that has a problem — it's your theory that needs updating.

Our generation is living through an unprecedented **collective great falsification**. It's not one belief that's shattering — it's the entire belief system that underpinned how society functions, being systematically falsified under the impact of AI.

This is painful. But it may also be a good thing — because only when the false is cleared away can the true have room to grow.

---

## I. What Did We Believe?

Before discussing what's being falsified, let's look at what our generation (post-80s, 90s, even 00s) actually believed.

These beliefs weren't things you actively chose. They were instilled by your environment from childhood — what your parents said, what your teachers said, what society assumed, what was shown on TV. They surrounded you like air; you weren't even aware you were "believing" anything, because they'd already been accepted as "common sense."

Let me list them:

1. **The Credential Faith**: Good degree = good job = good life
2. **The Employment Faith**: Find a good company, stay loyal, and the company will reward you
3. **The Property Faith**: Buying a house is life's most important investment — the earlier the better
4. **The Linear Faith**: Life is a straight line — junior → mid-level → senior → management, climbing step by step
5. **The Stability Faith**: Stability = safety; government jobs > private sector; big companies > small companies
6. **The Knowledge Faith**: Mastering specialized knowledge is your core competitiveness; more knowledge = more advantage
7. **The Scale Faith**: Bigger is better; big companies beat small ones; big teams beat small ones

These seven beliefs formed a complete "life operating system." You made choices accordingly: study desperately for college entrance exams, pick a "promising" major, fight your way into a big tech company after graduation, use your first paychecks for a down payment, then climb the ladder step by step, hoping to pay off your mortgage before retirement.

This system ran for thirty years and genuinely worked — for the previous generation.

Philosopher of science Thomas Kuhn proposed a concept in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* called the **"paradigm"** — not a single specific theory, but an entire underlying framework people use to understand the world and make judgments. During "normal periods," people solve problems within the paradigm, and everything proceeds in orderly fashion. But when anomalies accumulate to a certain point and the old paradigm can no longer explain an increasing number of realities, a **"paradigm shift"** occurs — the entire old framework is overturned and replaced by a new one.

Kuhn particularly emphasized: paradigm shifts are not gradual improvements, but **revolutionary ruptures**. People within the old paradigm don't slowly accept the new one — they resist, deny, and rage until reality shatters their last illusions.

What we're experiencing is a revolution in our life paradigm. Those seven beliefs above aren't seven independent opinions — they're seven components of a **complete paradigm**. They support and depend on each other: the credential faith supports the employment faith, the employment faith supports the property faith, and the stability faith locks them all together.

**When a paradigm begins to collapse, it doesn't just chip at one corner — it collapses entirely.**

## II. The Seven Falsifications

### Falsification One: Credentials ≠ Competitiveness

**Old assumption**: Academic credentials are the gold standard for talent screening. A degree from a top university = strong ability, high potential, worthy of investment.

**Reality's rebuttal**:

In the spring of 2026, big tech companies started another round of layoffs. Social media was again flooded with those familiar phrases: "Grateful for the years of growth at this company..." Among those laid off, graduates from elite universities were well represented. They discovered that the diploma they'd spent over a decade earning offered zero immunity from the layoff list.

Meanwhile, a young person who never attended university built a SaaS product with AI, earning monthly revenue that exceeded their annual salary.

Credentials are fundamentally a **signaling mechanism** — they tell employers "this person is probably decent." But when AI can evaluate someone's actual productive capability in minutes, when a person's GitHub repositories, product demos, and content portfolio can directly demonstrate "what I can do" — what use is this crude signal?

We're entering an era of **"Show me, don't tell me."** What you've built matters a hundred times more than where you graduated from.

**What's being falsified isn't education itself, but the assumption that "a piece of paper can protect you for life."** The real value of education lies in training your thinking and upgrading your cognition, not in that certificate.

### Falsification Two: Employment ≠ Security

**Old assumption**: You sell your time and skills to a company; the company gives you salary, benefits, a sense of belonging, and professional identity. This contract is long-term, stable, and mutually beneficial.

**Reality's rebuttal**:

Meta slashed 10,000 people in one stroke. Google laid off 12,000. Amazon cut 27,000. ByteDance, Alibaba, Tencent, and Baidu followed in turn. Small and medium businesses fared worse — you don't hear about their layoffs because they simply vanish.

You spent eight years at a big tech company, revising presentations until 2 AM, and one email told you to pack your things.

What happened? It's not that companies turned evil — it's that the **underlying equation** supporting the employment contract changed. Companies used to win through scale — more people meant more output. AI changed this equation. An engineer who uses Claude Code is ten times more efficient than one who doesn't. A content creator who masters AI workflows can produce in one day what used to take a week. What companies need is no longer "scale" but "density" — a small number of excellent people + powerful AI systems.

**The old contract no longer needs so many signatories.**

What's being falsified isn't "work," but the assumption that "being employed long-term by one company provides security."

### Falsification Three: Property ≠ The Optimal Asset

**Old assumption**: Buying a house is life's most important investment. It's a home, a marriage prerequisite, a social status symbol, a wealth appreciation tool, a source of security. Housing prices will always rise.

**Reality's rebuttal**:

Housing prices are falling. But that's just the surface. The deeper issue is that all the underlying conditions supporting high prices are shifting — population is declining, urbanization is slowing, remote work is spreading, and young people's insistence on "must buy a house" is loosening.

The logic of a 30-year mortgage rests on "I'll always have a job, and my salary will keep rising." When the employment faith is falsified, the property faith naturally begins to wobble. A single layoff can trigger a chain reaction — defaulting on payments, downgraded consumption, anxiety spreading to marriage and parenting.

Emptying six family members' savings and taking on 30 years of debt is a product of **certainty thinking** in an **age of uncertainty**. When a house no longer serves the function of "wealth appreciation," it returns to its true nature — a place to live. And there are many ways to obtain a place to live.

**What's being falsified isn't "having a home," but the assumption that "buying a house is the only correct choice."**

### Falsification Four: Linear Paths ≠ The Road to Success

**Old assumption**: Life is a ladder — junior → mid-level → senior → manager → director → VP. Don't make big mistakes, and when the time comes, you climb up one rung.

**Reality's rebuttal**:

AI first replaces the **middle layer**. Junior employees still have growth value, senior experts have irreplaceable judgment, but that large middle band of "execute + coordinate + relay messages" roles — AI does it faster, more accurately, without taking sick days or complaining.

What's more lethal is that the **half-life** of knowledge and skills is shrinking dramatically. Something you spent five years learning might be obsolete in two. You're a "senior React engineer" this year; next year the entire frontend might be replaced by AI generation.

Climbing a ladder assumes the ladder is fixed. But now the ladder itself keeps morphing, disappearing, and rebuilding.

The career path of the future isn't a ladder — it's a **jungle**. You need to constantly explore, pivot, and adapt, rather than climb along a fixed route.

**What's being falsified isn't "pursuing progress," but the assumption that "following a predetermined path will get you to the destination."**

### Falsification Five: Stability ≠ Safety

**Old assumption**: A stable job = a safe life. Civil service, public institutions, and state-owned enterprises are "havens where you'll never be unemployed."

**Reality's rebuttal**:

Government revenues are declining, headcounts are shrinking, and benefits are decreasing. AI's replacement of administrative and procedural work treats government and private sector alike. When an AI system can handle 90% of administrative approval processes, do you still need that many people behind the counter?

What is the essence of the "iron rice bowl"? It's trading **possibility** for **predictability** — you give up higher income and freedom in exchange for stability and safety. But when even the "iron rice bowl" isn't iron anymore, this trade becomes a bad deal: you gave up possibility but didn't receive the promised predictability.

The deeper issue is: **In a rapidly changing world, "stability" itself is the greatest risk.** Because stability means you don't need to learn, adapt, or change — and that's precisely the most dangerous state in an age of uncertainty.

**What's being falsified isn't "safety matters," but the assumption that "staying unchanged equals staying safe."**

### Falsification Six: Knowledge ≠ A Moat

**Old assumption**: Mastering specialized knowledge in a field is your core competitiveness. The more you know, the more irreplaceable you are.

**Reality's rebuttal**:

AI has democratized knowledge. Anyone can access expert-level knowledge in virtually any field within three seconds. The industry experience and professional barriers you spent a decade building? AI helps a newcomer scale those walls in a month.

The professional skills you took pride in — coding, design, translation, analysis — may be commoditized by AI tools within two years. This isn't hypothetical; it's already happening.

But there's a critical twist here. Philosopher of science Michael Polanyi proposed the concept of **"tacit knowledge"** in *Personal Knowledge* — "We know more than we can tell." You can ride a bicycle, but you can't precisely describe in words how you maintain balance. A veteran traditional Chinese medicine doctor taking a pulse can perceive subtle differences that no textbook can capture. An entrepreneur with ten years of experience can read a business plan and sense within three minutes where something's off.

AI crushes **explicit knowledge** — the kind that can be codified, searched, and standardized. But the tacit knowledge Polanyi described — knowledge embedded deep in the body, intuition, and experience — is precisely the part AI has the hardest time reaching.

So the real differentiator is no longer "what you know," but **"what you can do with knowledge"** — What good questions can you ask? What new things can you create by synthesizing knowledge across different domains? When AI gives you ten options, can you make the right call based on that inexplicable gut feeling?

**What's being falsified isn't "learning has value," but the assumption that "knowledge accumulation equals competitive advantage."**

### Falsification Seven: Scale ≠ Power

**Old assumption**: Bigger is stronger; winner takes all. Big companies have advantages over small ones; big teams beat small ones.

**Reality's rebuttal**:

A single person using AI built a product that beat a fifty-person team's product. The founder of Base44 reached an $80 million exit solo. A three-person indie game studio got more downloads than a major studio's AAA title. A one-person media account achieved more influence than an entire editorial team.

AI is a massive **"de-scaling" force** — it distributes capabilities that only large organizations once possessed to every individual. One person plus AI can build a usable product in a weekend.

**In the new order, the fundamental economic unit is no longer "the company," but "the individual + AI."**

**What's being falsified isn't "collaboration is powerful," but the assumption that "you must depend on a large organization to accomplish anything."**

## III. Why Now? Why All at Once?

You might ask: these assumptions have existed for decades — why are they all being falsified at the same time, right now?

Philosopher W.V.O. Quine proposed an elegant metaphor in *Two Dogmas of Empiricism*: the **Web of Belief**. He said our beliefs don't exist in isolation — they form an interconnected web. When beliefs at the periphery are hit by reality, you can make minor adjustments to preserve the whole web. But when the impact is large enough and strikes the web's core nodes directly, the entire web tears apart.

This is exactly what's happening. Those seven beliefs aren't seven independent strands — they're a web. AI isn't probing at the edges. **It struck the central node of the web directly — the basic assumption that "humans do things."**

AI is not an ordinary new technology. The internet changed how information flows but didn't change the fundamental nature of "humans doing things." AI is different — it directly replaces "humans doing things" itself.

- It eliminates companies' need for scale → Employment faith falsified
- It makes knowledge no longer scarce → Knowledge faith and credential faith falsified
- It gives individuals capabilities that previously required teams → Scale faith falsified
- It dramatically shortens the half-life of skills → Linear faith and stability faith falsified
- All of the above combined undermines the assumption that "future income will keep growing" → Property faith falsified

**AI is a falsification accelerator** — it didn't create these problems, but exposed the fragility of these already-flawed assumptions in an extremely compressed timeframe.

Looking back at the past four years:

- **2023**: ChatGPT reached 100 million users in two months, becoming the fastest-growing consumer application in history. But most people's reaction was "decent for writing a weekly report, not reliable for real work." At OpenAI's DevDay at year-end, Sam Altman announced weekly active users surpassed 100 million — 80% of Fortune 500 companies were already using it. Most people still hadn't realized what was happening.
- **2024**: AI began replacing entire teams. Klarna's AI customer service handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, covering 23 markets and 35 languages — equivalent to **700 full-time customer service agents** — with average resolution time dropping from 11 minutes to under 2. Duolingo cut 10% of its translation and content outsourcing team; AI took over their work. This was no longer a lab demo but real cost replacement.
- **2025**: Coding agents exploded. GitHub CEO Thomas Dohmke announced Copilot was involved in writing nearly **46% of code** on the platform, predicting AI would "soon" write 80%. Google CEO Sundar Pichai confirmed on an earnings call that over **30% of new code** at Google was AI-generated. Cursor's annualized revenue surged from $100 million to **$1.2 billion**, a 1,100% increase, with over half of Fortune 500 companies using it. Salesforce's CEO confirmed laying off **4,000 customer service staff**, with AI handling 50% of the work. In the US alone, over **55,000 people** were laid off due to AI in 2025.
- **2026**: Structural reshaping began. Block (Square's parent company) CEO Jack Dorsey cut **4,000 people — 40% of employees** in one stroke, publicly declaring: "AI tools have changed what it means to run a company. Fewer people, using the tools we're building, can do more and better." The stock surged 24% that day. He predicted: "Within a year, most companies will make the same structural adjustment." This was no longer isolated "optimization" — in early 2026 alone, over 45 CEOs publicly cited AI efficiency as the reason for layoffs.

This isn't winter. Winter passes. This is **tectonic movement**. After tectonic movement, rivers change course and the landscape is permanently altered.

## IV. After Falsification: What Is Being Validated?

This is the crucial chapter.

Many readers will feel despair at this point — if every belief has shattered, what can you still believe in?

Popper's student Imre Lakatos had an elegant answer to this. He divided theoretical systems into two kinds: **progressive research programs** and **degenerating research programs**. A degenerating program has one telltale sign — it constantly needs patches, exceptions, and special explanations to sustain itself. "Credentials are still useful, it's just that competition is fiercer now." "Big tech is still good, it's just a cyclical downturn." "Housing prices will rise again, it's just a temporary correction." Sound familiar? **When you need an ever-growing number of "it's just" qualifiers to maintain a belief, that belief is already degenerating.**

A progressive program is the opposite — it doesn't need patches; instead, it predicts and explains an ever-growing range of new phenomena.

But the point of falsification isn't to make you believe in nothing — it's to **liberate you from false assumptions so you can discover what genuinely holds true**. This is how science progresses: overthrowing a wrong theory isn't regression; it's one step closer to the truth.

In Lakatos's words: **Break free from the degenerating program and embrace the one that's progressing.**

So while old assumptions are being falsified, what new assumptions are being **validated** by reality?

### Validation One: The Era of Individual Empowerment Has Arrived

For the first time in history, an ordinary person can:

- Use AI to write code, without a computer science degree
- Use AI to create designs, without artistic training
- Use AI to produce content on par with professional teams
- Use AI for data analysis, without a statistics background
- Use AI to build products — one person is an entire company

The Industrial Revolution required capital to own machines. What does the AI Revolution require? A computer, an internet connection, and a few dozen dollars a month in subscription fees.

**The barrier to entry has never been this low. The ceiling has never been this high.** This isn't an assumption; it's being validated by countless examples right now.

### Validation Two: Connection Is More Resilient Than Employment

The dominant work model of the future isn't belonging to one company, but **simultaneously connecting with multiple projects, multiple teams, and multiple income streams**. In the morning you do product design for a startup; in the afternoon you provide AI consulting for another team; in the evening you run your own media channel.

This isn't "gig work." This is proactive, high-skill, compounding **connected work** — every collaboration builds reputation, portfolio, and network, and these assets belong to you.

Solo founders, freelance alliances, project-based micro-teams — these new organizational forms are rapidly validating an assumption: **Lightweight, flexible, people-centric organizations are better suited to this era than massive, rigid, position-centric ones.**

### Validation Three: Antifragility Is Safer Than Stability

In a fundamentally uncertain world, pursuing certainty is a strategy doomed to fail. The new paradigm being validated is **antifragility** — not "never getting knocked down," but "becoming stronger every time you're knocked down."

Antifragile life design:

- **Diversified income**: Don't put all your eggs in one basket
- **Transferable skills**: Don't tie yourself to a specific role or industry
- **Low fixed costs**: Travel light, preserve your options
- **Continuous learning**: Learning itself becomes a way of life

### Validation Four: Experiences and Connections Matter More Than Material Possession

As the cost of material production continues to decline, "owning" ceases to be a status signal. What becomes truly scarce:

- Meaningful experiences — a project that puts you in flow, a conversation that shifts your worldview
- Genuine human connections — three to five friends you can call at 3 AM
- A sense of continuous growth — being able to do today what you couldn't yesterday
- Autonomy — a sense of control over your time and life

**The more AI-driven the era becomes, the scarcer and more precious real human relationships become.**

### Validation Five: The Ability to Ask > The Ability to Answer

AI is the most powerful answer machine in history. But it needs humans to input the right questions.

Among the seven falsifications, "Knowledge ≠ A Moat" was overturned. The new assumption replacing it is: **The ability to define problems is the true core competitiveness of this era.**

People who can ask good questions are a hundred times more valuable than people who can answer them. Because AI can instantly answer any known question, but "what to ask" — that's something only humans can decide.

## V. A Personal Survival Guide for the Age of Great Falsification

Alright — old assumptions have been falsified, new assumptions are being validated. But you're not reading a textbook; you're a real person with a family, a mortgage, and children. Theory is beautiful, but reality is that you still have to go to work tomorrow and make your loan payment next month.

In the no-man's-land between old and new orders, how do you live?

### 1. Don't Panic — Falsification Isn't Destruction

Old assumptions being falsified doesn't mean your entire past was wrong. The knowledge you've acquired, the experience you've accumulated, the networks you've built — none of that has disappeared. It's just that their "valuation model" has changed. You need to extract and apply these assets in new ways, not discard them.

### 2. Protect Your Base, Free Up Space to Explore

Don't quit your job impulsively. Don't go all-in. Don't mix survival with exploration. First ensure your basic livelihood is secure, then use your spare time to explore new possibilities. This isn't cowardice — it's **wisdom**.

### 3. Build an AI-First Mindset

It's not about "learning a few AI tools" — it's about making AI your **first instinct** for thinking and doing. For any task, the first question should be: "Can AI help me with this?" Make AI an extension of your cognitive ability, infrastructure on the same level as your computer and phone.

This is an efficiency leap. My personal experience is that productivity increases at least fivefold. Not because I got smarter, but because I handed repetitive mental labor to AI and focused only on **judgment, decisions, and creation**.

### 4. Train Computational Thinking

In the AI era, the most essential human capability is **defining problems**. Computational thinking — problem decomposition, pattern recognition, abstract modeling — is the way to train this ability. You don't necessarily need to learn programming. Start by breaking down everyday problems: What groceries do I need? What are the steps? Which can be done in parallel? That's computational thinking in embryonic form.

### 5. Study Philosophy, Sharpen Judgment

When AI can handle more and more of the "how to do it," **"why do it" and "should we do it"** become humanity's last differentiating capabilities. Philosophy trains critical thinking, ethical judgment, and first-principles reasoning. A person without philosophical literacy using AI is like someone with no sense of direction on a skateboard — the faster you go, the further off course you drift.

### 6. Study Math, Understand the Fundamentals

Probabilistic thinking, statistical thinking, logical thinking — you don't need to become a mathematician, but you need to understand the language of the world's underlying logic. Understanding math means you won't treat AI as magic, but as a tool, knowing where its capability boundaries lie.

### 7. Build Things — Don't Hoard Anxiety

Bookmarking a thousand tutorials isn't learning — it's **hoarding anxiety**. There's only one real way to learn: **do**. Use AI to help you do one thing today — write an email, create a presentation, analyze a dataset. Between knowing and doing lies ten thousand instances of "just try it."

### 8. Play More, See More, Stay Curious

Innovation almost always comes from cross-pollination. Steve Jobs brought calligraphic aesthetics into computing. The more domains you're exposed to, the more "connection points" form in your brain. A playful mindset is itself a competitive advantage — curiosity-driven exploration is far more efficient than anxiety-driven cramming.

## VI. For the Children: Don't Educate with Falsified Assumptions

Education is the domain that most urgently needs attention in this great falsification.

Because the vast majority of parents are still using **already-falsified assumptions** to plan their children's futures — drilling relentlessly for exams, fighting to get into elite schools, choosing "promising" majors. They don't love their children any less; it's that their "navigation map" is outdated.

**Navigating with an old map will only lead your children to a destination that no longer exists.**

In the AI era, the core proposition of education has changed. From "equip the child with a valuable skill" to "raise the child to be someone AI cannot replace."

What can AI not replace?

- **Curiosity** — AI doesn't spontaneously ask questions, but people who ask good questions are far more valuable than those who answer them
- **Empathy** — AI can simulate emotions but cannot truly understand people
- **Aesthetics and taste** — AI can generate ten thousand images, but choosing which one is a human judgment call
- **Courage and decisiveness** — Making decisions amid uncertainty; the one pressing the button must be you
- **Cross-domain connecting ability** — Discovering hidden connections between different fields is the core of creativity

Therefore:

1. **Let children use AI early and extensively** — not studying AI, but **using** AI; the earlier they build collaborative intuition, the better
2. **Cultivate deep reading and independent thinking** — in an age of scarce attention, this is a true luxury
3. **Encourage hands-on creation** — the form doesn't matter; what matters is the complete experience of creating something from 0 to 1
4. **Expose children to the world** — different people, lifestyles, cultures, and values
5. **Fewer answers fed, more questions guided** — when a child asks "why," respond with "what do you think?"

## Conclusion: Great Falsification Is Great Liberation

Every era has its "unquestionable truths."

Medieval people believed geocentrism was absolute truth — until Copernicus falsified it. Industrial-age people believed handicrafts would never disappear — until the steam engine falsified that. Information-age people believed print media would always be mainstream — until the internet falsified it.

Our generation believed that credentials, property, iron rice bowls, and big companies were life's cornerstones. Now, AI is falsifying them.

This hurts. Because falsification means something you deeply believed was wrong, and decisions you made based on those beliefs may also have been wrong. The weight of sunk costs makes you want to hold on — "I've been walking this path for so long; it can't all be for nothing."

But falsification has never been destruction. **Falsification is liberation.**

The most iconoclastic thinker in the philosophy of science, Paul Feyerabend, wrote something in *Against Method* that unsettled the entire academic world: **"The only principle that does not inhibit progress is: anything goes."**

He wasn't advocating chaos. He was saying: **When you discover that old methodologies no longer work, don't rush to replace them with another set of standard answers.** Stay open, keep experimenting, maintain skepticism toward every established framework. Progress isn't jumping from one dogma to another — it's continuous exploration through falsification.

This is precisely the most precious gift of the Age of Great Falsification — not giving you a new set of "correct answers," but **completely liberating you to find answers that are truly your own**.
