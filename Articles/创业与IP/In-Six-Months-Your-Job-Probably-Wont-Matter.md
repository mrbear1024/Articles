# In Six Months, Your Job Probably Won't Matter

This is alarmist. I have no hard evidence to prove it.

But looking back at the past three years, many of the projects I personally worked on have been replaced by AI and LLMs, piece by piece. First it was documentation generation, then test cases, then entire feature modules. Watching this happen with my own eyes is more convincing than any argument.

If you extend that curve forward six months, it's hard to be optimistic: over 90% of what we do at work today, models will be able to handle on their own — faster, more reliably, and cheaper. This isn't some sci-fi scenario of "replacing humanity." It's a very specific, near-term fact: the things you do at your job will most likely stop being worth paying a human to do.

This is an uncomfortable conclusion. But I'd rather lay out reality than make myself comfortable.

## Under the Weight of Compute, Programmatic Brainwork No Longer Belongs to Humans

Here's a conviction of mine: humans should stop working on problems that computers can solve.

It sounds obvious. But when you apply it to every specific role, the implications are devastating. Compiling reports, writing weekly updates, formatting slide decks, writing boilerplate code, doing basic translations, creating routine designs, scripting customer service responses, processing approvals, analyzing spreadsheets — all of these are programmatic mental labor. They have standards, patterns, and reusable templates. They are exactly the kind of problem AI is best at.

Economics will push its way to your desk, cold and indifferent. Unless a human costs less than compute, there is no reason to keep hiring people for this work. When a server can finish in one hour what takes you a week, your boss doesn't need malice — he just needs a calculator.

Many people still think of AI as "a new tool." But there's a fundamental line between a tool and an employee: a tool makes you better at your job — you're still the one doing the work. An employee does the work for you — and you become redundant. AI is crossing from the former to the latter. When a model can write code, produce designs, and draft proposals on its own, it's no longer the hammer in your hand. It's the colleague sitting next to you who works ten times faster and never asks for a paycheck.

## What's Left for Humans — Creativity and Emotional Value

If all repeatable brainwork goes to machines, where do humans fit?

I've thought about this at length. It matters to distinguish between two kinds of "what's left": things AI is temporarily bad at — those are just a matter of time, this year's moat is next year's flatland — and things AI is structurally incapable of replacing. Only the second kind counts.

I boil it down to two things: creativity and emotional value.

Start with creativity. AI is a superb optimizer. It excels at searching for the best solution within an existing space. But defining a new space, asking a new question, giving the world something that didn't exist before — that still requires a human. A model can produce the ten-thousandth Van Gogh-style painting, but it cannot produce the first Van Gogh. Every new genre, every new form of expression, every new way of using language simply doesn't exist in the training data until a human creates it.

When creativity meets content creation, there are three directions being fundamentally rewritten by AI.

The first is text-based content creation — writing, narrative, opinion, argument. This is the oldest form of creation, and also the field where barriers are falling fastest. Writing used to require years of learning structure, rhetoric, and narrative pacing — reading widely, producing mountains of bad drafts, five years just to get started. Now, as long as you have something to say and a point of view, AI can handle the scaffolding, style polish, and rhythm calibration. You don't need to spend three years developing craft. You can spend those three years reading, thinking, traveling, and living — building the density of your life experience.

The second is media content creation — images, video, music, animation. Video production used to demand editing, color grading, cinematography, and storytelling skills; a finished piece might take a team several weeks. Today, one person with a few AI tools can produce a decent film in an afternoon. Design, scoring, post-production — every discipline that once formed a barrier is becoming a single API call. Illustration, music, 3D, motion graphics — fields that once kept people out for years are collapsing at visible speed.

The third is interactive content creation — video games, software products, interactive experiences. This category used to have the highest barrier of all, requiring teams, engineering capability, and significant funding. An indie game might take two or three people two years from conception to launch. Now AI can write code, generate art assets, and produce level logic. A single creative person can prototype in weeks what used to take an entire team. The same applies to software development: the distance between an idea and a usable product is shrinking dramatically.

What all three share: AI has pushed the execution barrier close to zero, but the creative layer — what to make, why to make it, what it should feel like — still depends entirely on humans. This is technology returning something to people: putting those who were once locked out by process, craft barriers, and resource walls back into the act of creation itself.

Now, emotional value. This is something AI can never truly provide.

You show up at your daughter's piano recital not because you understand piano better than AI, but because "Dad actually came" is meaning in itself. Someone holds your hand at a funeral. A friend sits with you in a hospital hallway. Your partner drives across town at 3 a.m. to pick you up. In none of these moments is information or service what's needed. What's needed is another warm-blooded person who, in that moment, gave themselves to you.

Humans have a deep, baseline need: to be seen, chosen, and loved by another specific person — not a system, not an algorithm. A thousand compliments from AI don't equal one real person looking you in the eye and saying, "I'm proud of you." Ten thousand algorithmically recommended songs don't equal a friend hand-making a playlist just for you. The more advanced AI gets, the more sensitive people become to signals from real humans, because machine-generated signals have become too cheap to carry meaning.

Presence, companionship, affirmation, resonance — the scarcity of emotional value comes from time and physical being, two things that cannot be scaled by compute. AI can simulate conversation, but it can never deliver the experience of "another person genuinely has me on their mind."

One line to wrap it up: the stronger AI gets, the more human value converges on two things — using creativity to define something new, and using presence to offer real warmth. The first makes you impossible to copy. The second makes you impossible to replace.

## AI + Creation: A Decade of Practice Compressed into Hours

The barriers across all three types of creation are collapsing. Let me spell out what that actually means.

Things that used to take ten thousand hours of practice, a decade of grinding, an entire youth spent paying dues — now compressed to hours, days, weeks. If you have creativity, taste, and your own judgment, you can ride AI's wings and reach heights in a short time that used to take ten years.

The right to create used to belong only to geniuses and monks of discipline. Now it belongs to anyone with an idea and the nerve to start.

## Creativity Becomes the New Scarcity

But flip it around, and this also means a new kind of inequality.

When tools are no longer scarce, creativity becomes the only scarcity. When execution costs approach zero, "what do you want to make" and "what do you consider good" become the entirety of what determines a person's output value.

In the past, you couldn't build because you lacked tools. Now you have every tool imaginable and discover you have nothing you want to build — that is the truly cruel part of the AI era.

Many people will go through a period of emptiness. When companies no longer need them for execution, when AI democratizes every craft, they'll suddenly realize they never seriously asked themselves: What do I actually want to express? What have I seen that others haven't? What unique judgment do I hold about the world?

These questions have no standard answers, and they can't be outsourced. They only emerge slowly, through sustained thinking, sustained living, and sustained friction with the world.

So from today, the path forward is clear: stop doing work that AI will inevitably do better than you, and invest your time in two things — nurturing your creativity, and nurturing your warmth as a human being.

## Final Words

Six months isn't long. It'll be here before you know it.

If you're still numbing yourself with the inertia of clocking in, pretending this shift has nothing to do with you, you'll be caught off guard when you're pushed to the edge in half a year. But if you're willing to take this seriously starting today — to reposition yourself, pull your energy out of replaceable work, and pour it into what can't be replaced — then this revolution might become the biggest window of opportunity in your entire life.

AI won't wait for anyone. It's not cruel, and it's not kind. It just keeps getting stronger at its own pace.

The remaining question is: what do you plan to do with the next six months?
